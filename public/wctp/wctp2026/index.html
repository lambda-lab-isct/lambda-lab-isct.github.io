WCTP2026

Workshop on Computation: Theory and Practice
November 25th — 27th, 2026
The Learning Resource Center, De La Salle Lipa, Lipa City, Batangas, Philippines

About

Computation should be a good blend of theory and practice. Researchers in the field should create algorithms to address real world problems putting equal weight to analysis and implementation. Experimentation and simulation can be viewed as yielding to refined theories or improved applications.

WCTP 2026 is the 15th workshop organized by Institute of Science Tokyo, Chitose Institute of Science and Technology, Kyoto Tachibana University, University of the Philippines-Diliman and De La Salle University-Manila that is devoted to theoretical and practical approaches to computation. It aims to present the latest developments by theoreticians and practitioners in academe and industry working to address computational problems that can directly impact the way we live in society.

WCTP 2026 will feature work-in-progress presentations of prominent researchers selected by members of its Program Committee who come from highly distinguished institutions in Japan and the Philippines. The presentation at the workshop will certainly provide high quality comments and discussion that future research can benefit from.

Invited Speakers

Engage AI: From Personalisation to Co-Regulation — AI Powered Nudging in Education

Dr. Rhodora Abadia
Dean Academic, College of Engineering and Information Technology, Adelaide University

Abstract

AI-driven nudging is already reshaping higher education, often faster than the policies meant to govern it. As these systems increasingly prompt students on what to do next, a question emerges: are they nudging, persuading, or becoming genuine partners in learning? This presentation uses EngageAI, a persuasive, AI-driven nudging system developed at Adelaide University, to argue that human-AI collaboration in education is moving beyond personalisation toward co-regulation.

EngageAI responds to a costly pattern: around week four, some online students begin to disengage, and those who do are far more likely to fail. The system identifies the activities most linked to success and sends timely metacognitive nudges that help students plan, monitor and reflect. Its AI is course-aware and probabilistic. It knows the difference between “no activity” and “activity we cannot see”, reports its own confidence, and asks not just who is at risk, but who will actually benefit from a nudge. Every nudge is reviewed by a teacher, and students can opt out.

But a system that shapes what students notice, do and value is no longer a neutral tool. Drawing on the ethics of persuasive technology, critiques of nudging and Value-Sensitive Design, we ask where support ends and manipulation begins, and whose goals, the learner’s or the institution’s, are encoded in the algorithm.

Our answer is co-regulation, in which AI acts as a partner rather than a persuader: learning is regulated jointly by learners, teachers and intelligent systems. This calls for new roles for educators, protection of teacher agency, and institutional policies that recognise AI as a persuasive actor.

Navigating Artificial Intelligence in Higher Education: Transforming Teaching, Research, and Innovation

Dr. Raphael Gonda
Faculty, Department of Information Technology, De La Salle University; Head, Center for ICT for Development (CITe4D)

Abstract

The rapid adoption of generative artificial intelligence (AI) is reshaping teaching, research, and innovation in higher education. This study examines academic discourse surrounding generative AI to identify how educators and academic communities perceive its opportunities, concerns, and implications for institutional practice. Using computational text-mining approaches, including topic modeling and sentiment analysis of publicly available academic discussions on social media. The study maps dominant narratives related to AI-enabled learning support, research productivity, creativity, academic integrity, bias, data privacy, and unequal access.

Findings indicate that academic discourse reflects both optimism regarding AI’s capacity to support learning and research and concern regarding responsible use, overreliance, and the changing role of educators. These discussions suggest that higher education institutions need to move beyond reactive restrictions and develop context-sensitive approaches that strengthen AI literacy, ethical governance, assessment design, and human-centered innovation.

The study provides the foundation for a discussion on responsible generative AI use in higher education. It emphasizes the need for educators, researchers, and students to take ownership of how they use AI. This includes applying critical judgment, verifying outputs, acknowledging AI assistance where appropriate, and remaining accountable for the learning and research outcomes produced.

Submission

Submissions related to the topics of the workshop are very much welcome. Submissions that relate results from other areas to the workshop topics are also encouraged. Submitted papers should preferably be typeset in LaTeX2e using the style file. We recommend the usual page limits of around 15 pages.

Any submission should include the session name, the title of the paper, names and affiliations of authors, an abstract of 70-150 words, and the contact author's name, phone number, fax number, and email address. The session names are as follows:

  • Theoretical Approaches to Computations,
  • Practical Approaches to Computation,
  • Computation for Community-based Applications,
  • Computation Contributing to the Health and Medical Care Field.

Submissions must be in English. The paper must be unpublished and not submitted for publication elsewhere, including journals and proceedings of other symposia or workshops. One author of each accepted paper should be able to present it at the workshop. All submissions should be in PDF file format and uploaded via the EasyChair Conference System.

Peer-Review Policy

WCTP2026 is committed to ensuring the highest level of academic integrity, which includes a thorough and rigorous peer-review process.

  1. Submission: All submitted papers will be checked for relevance to the conference themes and scope. The submission should not have been previously published or under review elsewhere.
  2. Initial Screening: Each submission will undergo an initial screening for formatting, language quality, and adherence to submission guidelines.
  3. Single-Blind Review: Submissions passing the initial screening will then undergo a single-blind peer-review process. In this process, the identities of the authors are visible to the reviewers, while the reviewers remain anonymous to the authors. Submissions are evaluated based on originality, technical soundness, clarity of presentation, relevance to the conference themes, and potential impact on both theoretical and practical aspects of computer science.
  4. Reviewer Assignment: Each submission is typically reviewed by at least two expert reviewers within the field. The review panel is selected based on their expertise, and any potential conflicts of interest are considered and managed.
  5. Revision and Resubmission: Based on the reviews, papers may be accepted, rejected, or returned to the authors with requests for revision and resubmission. Authors must address the reviewers' comments systematically in their revision and provide a detailed response to the reviewers' comments.
  6. Final Decision: The final decision regarding acceptance of a paper is made by the Program Committee, considering the reviewers' feedback and the paper's contribution to the conference's diversity and balance.

Our peer-review process is designed to ensure that all accepted papers meet high standards of quality, originality, and relevance to the conference themes. By submitting a paper to WCTP2026, authors agree to undergo this process.
